What is Islaa7?
Islaa7 (إصلاح) is a government-run digital platform designed for the UAE that tackles one of the most overlooked environmental problems: the habit of throwing away things that could simply be fixed. The name comes from the Arabic word for "repair," with the number 7 cleverly replacing the Arabic letter ح — a bilingual touch that reflects the project's UAE identity. Instead of discarding broken appliances, furniture, garments, or even small household items like a pot with a cracked handle, residents use the Islaa7 app to book a repair. It's simple, affordable, and backed by the government.
The project was created by a team of Year 9 students — Omar, Nasser, Kamil, Abdelrahman, and Ibrahim — as part of the GEMS Global Innovation Challenge 2025–26, aligned with UN Sustainable Development Goal 12: Responsible Consumption and Production.

Why does it exist?
The UAE has one of the highest per capita waste generation rates in the world. In Abu Dhabi alone, each person produces around 1.8 kg of waste every single day. A community like Al Raha Beach, with roughly 120,000 residents, generates an estimated 79,000 tonnes of municipal waste per year. Of that, around 16,000 tonnes are items — furniture, appliances, textiles, utensils — that could realistically be repaired or refurbished rather than thrown away.
The root problem isn't just logistics — it's mindset. Economically comfortable families often discard items at the first sign of wear, not because they can't afford to fix them, but because finding a trustworthy repair person is inconvenient, prices are unclear, and there's no system encouraging them to try. Studies show that a 1% increase in median income leads to roughly 1.86 kg of extra household waste per person per year — meaning as the UAE grows wealthier, waste grows with it unless a structural solution is in place. Islaa7 is that solution.

How does it work?
The experience is designed to be as simple as ordering a taxi. A user opens the app, signs in with their UAE Pass account (since this is a government service), and sets up a basic profile with their location and contact details. When something breaks, they select the repair category — appliances, furniture, or garments — upload a photo, describe what's wrong, and choose a convenient date and time. An AI system, powered by the Groq API running a large language model called ChatGPT OSS 120B, analyses the photo instantly to identify the type of damage and estimate the likely repair cost. A verified, skilled technician is then matched to the job and dispatched — similar to how ride-sharing apps assign drivers.
The target is for 90% of repairs to be completed within 7 working hours. Workers set their own prices based on materials and labour. The platform takes a 7% commission, and the worker receives the remaining 93%. Payment is held in escrow until the user confirms the repair is satisfactory, protecting both sides.

Who does the repairs?
Islaa7 doesn't hire or train a brand new workforce. Instead, it onboards existing skilled workers — tailors, appliance technicians, carpenters, handymen — who register through a worker portal on the platform. Each worker goes through an admin verification and approval process before they can accept jobs. Once verified, they receive job alerts for nearby requests, accept on a first-come basis, and earn commission per repair. Workers who fall below a 4-star rating lose access to the platform, keeping quality high.
This model mirrors how Uber works, but for repairs. It gives small business owners and independent tradespeople a flexible extra income stream while keeping government recruitment and training costs low.

The rewards system
To make repair feel genuinely rewarding rather than a chore, Islaa7 runs a points programme. Users earn points for every item they repair. Those points can be redeemed for cinema tickets, meal vouchers, discounts on future repairs, and other benefits. On top of that, the first three repairs on the platform are completely free, and every three paid repairs earns one free repair. This is designed to pull in residents who might be hesitant at first — once they try it, the hope is that repair becomes a habit.
